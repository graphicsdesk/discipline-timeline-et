Many Called Columbia’s 2025 Expulsion of Nonviolent Student Protesters Unprecedented. But, 89 Years Ago, Columbia Expelled an Anti-Nazi Peacenik.

By Maya Henry, Yusuf Perwez, Dora Gao, Daksha Pillai

Graphics by Emily Ta

February 18, 2026

Saumya Chaudhry / Illustrations Editor

In February 2025, Barnard expelled two students for their alleged participation in a class disruption weeks prior, protesting Avi Shilon’s History of Modern Israel class.

The expulsions were monumental: Nationally, they marked the first known expulsions tied to college students protesting the war in Gaza. On Columbia’s campus, they marked something else: the first time students had been expelled for nonviolent political protest in 89 years.

In 1936, Columbia Dean Herbert Hawkes expelled Robert Burke, an anti-Nazi peacenik protesting the University’s increasingly friendly relationship with Nazi Germany. Burke was removed from the University through far different means than those protesting the war in Gaza in recent years: He had no hearing, no suspension period, and there were no Rules of University Conduct or University Judicial Board to speak of.

Despite Columbia’s robust protest history, no disciplinary action compares to the severity of Burke’s treatment or the suspensions and expulsions that have swept campus since protests over the war in Gaza erupted in fall 2023.

The Eye reviewed dozens of documents stored in Columbia’s University Archives collection from Burke’s expulsion and four other significant campus movements—in 1986, 1972, 1985, and 1996—to piece together the last expulsion of a student in modern Columbia history and to examine how the University’s disciplinary responses have become more severe over the decades. In the archives and interviews with professors, The Eye found that the University’s recent response to address protests and occupations on campus with expulsions is a departure from Columbia’s previous disciplinary practices.

The University did not respond to The Eye’s numerous requests for comment.

Modern historians have raised concerns about the severity of Columbia’s recent disciplinary action. “Nothing in Columbia’s earlier history of discipline” compares “to the extraordinary harshness of the penalties given to the students who participated in the 2024 takeover of Hamilton Hall or the protest in Butler Library in May 2025,” Kim Phillips-Fein, Robert Gardiner-Kenneth T. Jackson professor of history, wrote in a statement to Spectator.

Burke, for his part, raised similar concerns 89 years prior, cautioning in a letter to his attorney amid his expulsion hearing that “If Columbia can get away with this expulsion, freedom of thought and expression on every campus in the country will be endangered. The unwritten threat of administrative discipline will force more and more students to silence.”

***

Robert Burke, known to friends and classmates as Bob, was, among other things, an amateur boxing champion from Youngstown, Ohio, and president-elect of his Columbia College class. He was also a stark peacenik against Adolf Hitler, who by 1936 had begun to challenge the post-World War I order across Europe.

Hitler had, notably, already begun his crackdown on universities across Germany. Germany’s University of Heidelberg was ground zero. Starting in 1933, Heidelberg dismissed hundreds of faculty and students who were Jewish or suspected of having communist or socialist leanings. Professors bookended lectures with “Heil Hitler” and Heidelberg renamed its physics institute in honor of physicist and Nazi Philipp Lenard, who dismissed the work of Albert Einstein as “Jewish physics.”

Heidelberg students—or rather, those who remained—participated in large swaths in a youth-backed movement against “the un-German spirit.” On May 18, 1933, they staged a book burning in front of Schurman Hall in support of Hitler’s rise.

In Morningside Heights, and across college campuses in the United States, students were less cheerful.

When Heidelberg invited leaders from top universities across the world in February 1936 to join the university for its 550th year anniversary celebration the following summer, some universities, led by the University of Oxford, the University of Birmingham, and the University of Cambridge, were quick to refuse.

Columbia was not one of them.

Instead, on February 28, 1936, Columbia publicly announced its acceptance of the invitation.

The archives piece together a story of the fervent protests that followed in the month after.

Spectator sent a cable to then-University President Nicholas Butler, CC 1882, 1883, 1884, urging him to rescind the University’s acceptance; Columbia’s American Student Union chapter and Columbia Student Board voted to oppose the University’s participation; a petition against the acceptance circulated throughout the University, garnering signatures from notable faculty, including Nobel Prize winner Harold Urey; and Columbia’s Teachers Union demanded Butler reconsider the acceptance.

Margaret Cummings, then-editor of The Teachers College News, wrote: “That any reputable educator should want to attend such a celebration when the true voice of German culture is mute is unbelievable.”

Ultimately, the groups formed a committee; behind them were over 1,000 students and faculty members who signed a petition demanding rejection of the invitation.

On March 30, 1936, the committee and Butler met. In an affidavit written on behalf of Burke months later, a student who was in the meeting wrote that Butler seemed unfamiliar with the very nature of the invitation—was it from the University of Heidelberg? Was it from the German government and, thus, Hitler? Butler shared his deep concern for the future of Germany, the student wrote, and ridiculed a recent poll that said 98.87 percent of Germans voted for Hitler. The University president was personally and professionally against Hitler, he assured the students.

If Butler’s further study were to discover that the celebration would be in any way complimentary to the Third Reich, he would not only reject the invite, but let the involved and concerned students know, he said.

Academics of the modern day, like Steven Remy, a scholar of modern German history and professor at Brooklyn College, are less forgiving of Butler’s seeming oblivion. “Here’s a member of the American elite, an educated, intelligent man at the head of a prominent, powerful institution,” Steven Remy told The Eye, describing Butler. “He’s somebody who could, if he really wanted to, have an accurate idea of what was really going on inside German universities. He could have gotten all the information he wanted, but he didn’t want it. He only wanted to see what he wanted to see.”

Butler refused to see the celebration as a threat. So, a rejection never came—not after Nazi Minister of Propaganda Joseph Goebbels was announced as a keynote speaker, not after four American universities publicly denounced their early acceptances, and not after the committee urged Butler, again, to reconsider.

“There were people who fully understood at the time what was going on. And if you wanted to know what was going on in German universities, it wasn’t a secret,” Steven Remy said.

At Columbia, hundreds of students “understood.” So, they escalated. At the helm of their movement was Burke.

***

Yellowed and brittle copies of Spectator and New York Times columns from 1936 are mostly in agreement over what happened next.

On May 12, 1936, some 300 students assembled on the South Lawn to stage a mock book burning of their own. The few photos from the event show students smiling widely, holding signs—“On to Heidelberg–Bring Your Brass Knuckles,” “Nicholas Murray Butler Diddles While Books Burn”—mocking Butler, with hands raised in Nazi salutes.

Among the texts burned were a Manhattan telephone directory—“because it is full of Jewish names,” student Paul Kolisch declared—and the Contemporary Civilization syllabus—“because civilization has changed.” Texts by Butler and Hawkes were lit as well.

As the books burned, Burke called for students to “go over to Butler’s house for tea and biscuits.” Standing outside of the president’s house, Burke and other students rallied the crowd, congratulating Butler on his election to the Reichstag and shaming the president for his refusal to negotiate.

“Nicky, and I hope you hear this too, you can send a representative to Heidelberg but let it be known that he is not the choice of the student body,” Burke finished his speech.

A week later, Hawkes summoned Burke to a meeting. He accused Burke of being, among other things, the leader of a demonstration that was “in exceedingly poor taste” and beneath Columbia’s standard.

Amid the Burke-Hawkes talks, students again pushed Butler to reject the invitation to visit Heidelberg. “The public has yet to learn that our academic relationships have no political implications of any kind,” Butler wrote in a May 29 letter defending his decision to frustrated students.

By June 2, Hawkes and Burke met again, though the second meeting was considerably more tense than the first. Burke was advised by a faculty member to apologize to Hawkes again to placate him, but a third meeting and another apology never happened.

On June 16, Burke was dismissed from the University.

Burke loved Columbia, he reiterated in a letter to Hawke over the summer. “Those aspects I disliked I protested against in the hope that some change might be accomplished. This, to me, was loyalty to Columbia,” he wrote.

By the time Burke had apologized, Columbia professor Arthur Remy, whom Butler had sent to Heidelberg on behalf of the University, had already strolled across the German university’s campus. Arthur Remy had described the celebration—which an extensive collection of black-and-white photos and videos show featured large gold swastikas, a bronze statue of the Nazi eagle, and Nazi soldiers adorned in all black—as “very enjoyable.”

In an undated and unsigned press release, Columbia, Harvard University, and Yale University presented a unified front in the aftermath of their attendance, decrying the antagonism they bore witness to at the celebration. But, the damage—Columbia attending a Nazi propaganda event and Burke being expelled—had been done.

***

The following semester, Burke was prohibited from registering for classes.

Students, backed by the American Students Union, held all-night pickets outside Butler’s home. At colleges across the country, and at the City College of New York in particular, chapters of the American Student Union adopted resolutions calling for Burke’s reinstatement.

As hundreds of Columbia students protested Burke’s expulsion, chanting “Butler wants Hitler, we want Burke,” Columbia revised its policy on rioters on campus: For the first time, they would not be immune to arrest.

Administration at all levels were not deterred.

In a letter to the Burke Defense Committee, Butler wrote that, “at this time in the academic year when every waking hour is under severest pressure, there could be no possible advantage in my taking time to hear orally your plea for the readmission of Robert Burke to Columbia College.”

In response, Burke announced to students gathered outside Hamilton Hall that his attorney, Arthur Hays, had filed a motion with the Supreme Court to reinstate him, arguing that the University agreed to accord him all the rights and privileges granted to enrolled students when they admitted him—his immediate expulsion on June 16, 1936, it alleged, violated such rights and privileges.

Ahead of the lawsuit, attorneys defending Columbia compiled evidence proving that updated University statutes regarding academic discipline had been published and circulated prior to the protest on May 12 that sparked Burke’s expulsion.

The attorneys worked to build a case of bad character against Burke; utilizing Spectator clippings and a detailed log of his travels since his departure from Columbia, Columbia’s lawyers embedded ongoing litigation against Burke for the trouble he’d found himself in in the months following his expulsion in the thesis of their argument against readmission.

After months of delays, the lawsuit was dismissed. Calls for Burke’s reinstatement proved equally unsuccessful.

And then, Burke, as both a student and a flashpoint of First Amendment rights, disappeared from Columbia—or, at least, from Columbia’s archives.

***

A post-Burke Columbia continued to discipline political protesters—but, until recent history, stopped short of expulsion.

On April 23, 1968, nearly 100 students occupied Hamilton Hall, renaming it “Malcolm X Liberation College.”

The protesters had two core demands: cut ties with the Institute for Defense Analyses, a U.S. military research organization, and end the construction of a gym being built on the public land of Morningside Park, with separate entrances for students and Harlem residents.

The 1968 protesters occupied a total of five buildings: Hamilton Hall, Low Library, Avery Hall, Fayerweather Hall, and Mathematics Hall. When the New York Police Department cleared the buildings on April 30, 1968, 712 people were arrested, and 148 were injured. The violence of the sweeps was intense: Four faculty members received severe head wounds, and an infirmary was set up in Philosophy Hall that treated 35 people the next morning.

By the fall of that year, 21 students remained suspended for their participation in the protests. But months later, the Joint Committee on Disciplinary Affairs rescinded all distributed sanctions on the spring protesters. Unlike Burke’s landmark court case, suspended students were also allowed to reapply to Columbia as part of this decision.

Robert Newton, a lecturer in sustainability science who observed the 1968 Columbia protests as a high school student working with a documentary film collective called Newsreel, described vast differences between the University’s intensity and disciplinary response to the 1968 protests and the 2024 and 2025 protests over the war in Gaza.

“What the students did in 1968 was much, much more disruptive, more aggressive,” Newton told The Eye. “Their attitudes were more arrogant. Students from SDS were rifling through the president’s desk, smoking his cigars, hanging out a window smoking his cigars. They took files from the president’s office, and just dumped the files, made piles of them.”

Newton contrasted the conduct of the 1968 protesters with those in 2024.

“To compare that to 1968, you’d have to say that these students—our current batch of students in 2024—were, if anything, more principled, more calm, more peaceful, more deliberate, more circumspect in their messaging,” Newton said. “I mean, with very few exceptions, you’d have to say what they were doing was much more disciplined and within the bounds of civil discourse than what the students did in 1968.”

***

In April 1972, over 60 students and community members once again occupied Columbia buildings—Hamilton, Kent Hall, and Lewisohn Hall—demanding the resignation of five Columbia faculty members from the IDA.

At its peak, the protest swelled to between 1,000 and 2,000 students. Several more students were arrested in the following weeks.

The UJB initially issued sanctions to 48 students; Only three of the charged students were convicted at a disciplinary hearing, but all were later pardoned by the University.

But soon Hamilton would again be taken over, and a new batch of sanctions would be sent.

In the spring of 1985, protesters, calling on the University to divest from companies that did business in apartheid South Africa, occupied the building, renaming it “Mandela Hall.”

The spectre of police violence in 1968 hung over the University’s response in 1985, with then-University President Michael Sovern, CC ’53, Law ’55, reportedly stating in front of faculty that he would try not to call on NYPD officers to clear Hamilton.

The blockade ultimately concluded without police intervention.

Similar to 1968, discipline against the protesters initially charged with rules violations ended up light by the time the disciplinary process came to a close: Students whose degrees were previously withheld would be allowed to graduate, and sentences of all of the building occupiers were reduced to one semester of disciplinary warning.

No suspensions were handed out against the participants despite the three-week length of the blockade.

Then another major protest came in 1996, this time a hunger strike of four students demanding the creation of an ethnic studies department at Columbia.

When the demand was rejected by the University president at the time, 125 students occupied Low Library following a mass rally at the Sundial. The next morning, police entered and arrested 22 students on trespassing and fire code violation charges. All protesters were later released on bail.

After another two weeks of fasting and a five-day occupation of Hamilton, protesters and administrators reached an agreement on April 15. The University agreed to many of the protesters’ demands. The Center for the Study of Ethnicity and Race would be subsequently established three years later in 1999.

The University also pledged not to take any form of disciplinary action against the students who occupied Hamilton. Ultimately, the 1996 demonstrations concluded through negotiation between students and administrators.

The 1968, 1972, 1985, 1996, and 2024 protests all involved the occupation or blockade of buildings, and all involved hundreds of participants each, at a minimum. With the exception of 1985 and 2024, protesters in the other three demonstrations occupied more than one building during the course of the demonstrations—up to five in 1968. Demonstrators also resorted to more intense tactics beyond occupation, including the 1968 protesters taking a dean hostage for a day, and 1972 protesters clashing with police on the streets.

The University ultimately reversed or reduced sentences in four of the five protests. Not every student arrested for taking part in the 2024 pro-Palestinian campus protests faced disciplinary action beyond interim suspension—but those who did faced multiyear suspensions, expulsions, and degree revocations with no reported retraction.

The Rules of University Conduct—which govern the kind of behavior that warrants discipline—were created following the 1968 protests, and amended throughout the years. The last major change occurred in 2015, when the sanctions were broadened to include less severe punishments like warnings and community service.

Then, on March 21, 2025, a new set of changes to the rules stripped students’ right to request an open hearing, be granted a time frame for their disciplinary process, and protest inside academic buildings. The University Senate’s jurisdiction over the Rules was removed, too.

“They have insulated the disciplinary process from any shared governance whatsoever,” Newton said, referring to the University administration’s changing attitudes toward discipline following the 2024 protests. “They’re moving, if anything, in the wrong direction.”

Phillips-Fein provided a potential reason for what she believes is the University’s heightened discipline in response to the 2024-25 protests compared to past protests.

“Today, it seems that many in the University have adopted the position of some of those outside of the University: that student protesters are dangerous,” Phillips-Fein wrote. “This reflects a very different understanding of what universities are and what they are meant to accomplish.”

***

Eighty-nine years after Burke’s expulsion, the history between Columbia and the Third Reich remains largely unaddressed.

Butler retired as University president in 1945, the same year the Third Reich fell. Calls for an honorary degree to be granted to Burke, and for Columbia to grapple with its friendly relationship with the early days of Nazi Germany, have far outlasted him.

Half a century later, in 2008, then-University President Lee Bollinger, Law ’71, was repeatedly invited to speak on a panel organized by the David S. Wyman Institute for Holocaust Studies to address Columbia’s history with Nazism. Bollinger declined.

In contrast, other universities that sent delegates to the Heidelberg celebration have acknowledged the history, however ugly. The University of Michigan, for its part, has some 1,100 words dedicated to the university’s unfortunate acquiescence to Hitler’s earliest celebration of Aryanism in its school-funded Heritage Project, which details Michigan history—good and bad.

Kim Clarke, who founded and ran the Heritage Project for its duration and serves as an executive speechwriter in the office of the president at the university, described the vitality of the project, acknowledging the university’s prior missteps. “Universities, we get all puffed up about our rankings and our athletic teams, and that’s fine. School spirit’s important,” she said. “But somebody’s got to say, ‘Look, we really screwed up in this case.’”

For the University of Michigan, the “screw-up” mirrored Columbia: sending a professor, Aloysius Gaiss, to Germany on behalf of the then-president, Alexander Ruthven, despite intense protest from students.

Before the recent efforts of the Heritage Project, Michigan’s history with Heidelberg was seemingly lost to time. In New York City, Burke has largely been forgotten in Columbia’s long, storied, and documented history of student activism.

To this day, Burke, long dead, has not received an honorary diploma from the University.

In the past two years, as Columbia’s campus has been engulfed in protest over the war in Gaza and claims of rampant antisemitism on campus, there have been few references by the University to the fight against the Nazism that consumed Columbia in the late 1930s.

In July 2025, Rules Administrator Gregory Wawro announced that more than 70 students had been suspended or expelled for their involvement in a May 7, 2025, Butler Library demonstration. Among the reasons cited for discipline were the disturbances and vandalism that participating students inflicted. In a video acting University President Claire Shipman, CC ’86, SIPA ’94, released after the NYPD cleared Butler Library and arrested students that day, Shipman said, “Violence and vandalism, hijacking a library—none of that has any place on our campus. These aren’t Columbia’s values.”

Among the vandalism Wawro and Shipman decried—the signs, stickers, and graffiti that visually defined May 7, 2025—were scrawlings in green paint on tables in the Lawrence A. Wien Reference Room that read, “Butler was a Nazi.”👁
